Symbol frequencies · live

Twenty of the 24 symbols are used; 2nw alone is 11 of 87. Four never appear. For 87 letters of real English, 20 distinct letters sits at the 38th percentile — ordinary.

The rotation test — directions · live

why

A 45° turn moves a glyph to the next direction but keeps its ring. So if the key advances regularly — every glyph, every second glyph, whatever the step — each plaintext letter is smeared across all eight directions, and the eight counts are pushed towards 87/8 ≈ 10.9 whatever the key or the fill.

result

Dorabella's counts are 10 8 9 16 15 3 4 22: χ² = 26.6 on 7 degrees of freedom. In a Monte Carlo of 40,000 simulated rotating-snowflake ciphertexts (English plaintext, random fill) only 12 were this lumpy: p = 0.0003. Rotation also predicts 0.81 unused cells; Dorabella has 4.

A regular rotating key is ruled out. The test needs no key, no solver and no corpus, so nothing later can overturn it. Irregular, text-driven schedules escape the argument and stay open.

A repeating key? — periodic IC and Kasiski

The standard test for a repeating-key (Vigenère-type) cipher: split the stream into p interleaved cosets and measure the index of coincidence inside them. A genuine key of length p raises it at p and at every multiple. Measured on the flat stream, so it does not care whether the note is three rows or one string (lab/structure.py, 400 shuffles per period).

No period shows elevated coset IC (none reaches the 97.5th percentile of its shuffles). That is evidence against a repeating-key polyalphabetic cipher of any length up to 22. Kasiski agrees: repeated substrings give 25 bigram spacings and only 2 trigram spacings, the longest repeat is three glyphs (2nw 2s 2n, spacing 24), and the spacings' common factors (2, 4, 3, 5, 8, 11…) point at no single key length.

Vowels — Sukhotin's algorithm · live

In natural language the vowels fall out of adjacency structure alone: vowels and consonants alternate. Sukhotin's algorithm recovers that set without any key.

A port of lab/analysis.py, including its tie-break. Descriptive only — I never calibrated it against English at this length, so it is a direction to look in, not a result.

Can any substitution make it read?

An annealing solver (lab/solver.py, blended 3- and 4-gram English model) attacks Dorabella under each reading order, and the same solver at the same effort attacks 40 genuine English ciphertexts of 87 letters. Matched effort was the hard-won part — the history is on [08].

A plain substitution of English is excluded. Every transform sits 7.5 to 9.6 standard deviations below real English solved the same way. This is not a solver failure: on the same controls it recovered a median of 87 of 87 letters. Once the separation is this large the percentile saturates at 0 and stops being informative — the z-score is the number to quote.

The saved report's closing verdict line (“bottom 5%… disfavours…”) was printed by an older verdict branch that did not yet look at z; the script now prints “excluded” for z < −5. The numbers above are unchanged. The best “plaintext” is what a solver squeezes out of 87 glyphs — English-looking fragments, no message.
