The argument in six lines

1. Elgar's alphabet is 24 glyphs — one, two or three arcs facing one of eight directions. His own 1920s notebook draws it on a clock face: radius for the number of arcs, angle for the direction. My snowflake grid is the same object, so the key is a polar diagram.

2. I transcribed the note myself, glyph by glyph, and flagged nine glyphs I could not decide. The resolved reading agrees with a published 1:1 reading at 82/87 with zero letter collisions — a shuffled null averages 28.7, p < 5×10⁻⁵. The transcription is sound.

3. My first idea — a key that turns one step per glyph — is dead. A 45° turn keeps the ring, so a regular rotation forces the eight direction counts towards 10.9 each whatever the key. Dorabella's are 10 8 9 16 15 3 4 22: χ² = 26.6, and 40,000 simulated rotating ciphertexts give p = 0.0003. No key, no solver, no corpus needed.

4. It looks like language: index of coincidence, entropy, doubled glyphs, repeated bigrams and the letter-frequency profile all sit inside the range of 87 letters of real English.

5. But it does not read like English. Solved at the same effort as 40 real-English controls, every reading order lands 7.5 to 9.6 standard deviations below them — while the same solver recovers a median 87 of 87 letters on the controls. Hauer et al. (2021) and Wase (2023) reached the same conclusion independently.

6. English frequencies with non-English order is the fingerprint of a second layer (a rearrangement, or a key that moves irregularly) or of a plaintext that is not ordinary English. I built a long-running search for the first branch that is designed to say “nothing” loudly rather than “solution” softly. Nothing I have run has produced a reading.

What this is

In late August 2026 I spent a few intense days on the Dorabella cipher. It started as a hunch — the 24 glyphs are eight directions times three sizes, so perhaps the key is a snowflake that turns while you write — and grew into my own transcription, a Python research lab, a browser toolkit, and a server search built to run for months. Along the way I read everything I could find about Elgar, his other ciphers, and every published attempt.

This site keeps all of it, sorted: what I read, what I did, and — on its own page — every attempt with an honest verdict on whether it was worth the time.
